2. THE STORY
------------
In July 2022 I called an HVAC company to diagnose my air conditioner and agreed to a diagnostic fee of about
$100. On that call, after I described the problem, the person I spoke with said it sounded like it could
be the thermostat. I said I didn't think so, because the problem had only just started.

The next day, a technician showed up and found the issue was a disconnected thermostat wire, reconnected it,
and afterwards came to me and told me he was done. I thanked him, and he said they would bill me. No amount
was mentioned.

About two weeks later, I received an invoice for $500. I figured it was a mistake. I had only agreed to a
diagnostic, and I assumed reconnecting the thermostat wire was part of it, since it takes about as much time
and effort as plugging something into a wall outlet, and I wasn't told about a separate repair or charge
before or after the technician did the work.

I called the company to sort out the mix-up. The owner told me, as I wrote at the time, that the reason he
charged me $500 was that on that first call I'd told him I didn't think the problem was the thermostat. I
described it in writing twice: in a July 2022 email to the creditor, and in my August 2022 written dispute
to the collection agency.

I filed a complaint with the Idaho Attorney General, paid $100 in person, and disputed the rest. The creditor
assigned the roughly $400 balance to a collection agency. On authorization, its written response to the Attorney
General offered one explanation: that its technician understood I had "tacitly agreed to the repair."
The Attorney General's office then closed the complaint, noting that the business was unwilling to resolve it.

I sent the agency a detailed written dispute in August 2022, pointing to that response. Its answer to my
counterclaim admits forwarding that dispute to the creditor. In 2026 the agency and its attorney sent new
demands; I disputed again in writing. The attorney replied with a copy of the invoice and sued nearly four
years later, in May 2026.

c) The updated invoice lists seven troubleshooting tasks under the diagnostic. Task 7 is the
   technician finding and correcting the wiring problem.

   The invoice then separately charges about $500 on a wiring-repair line item, described generically

d) The creditor's written response to my Idaho Attorney General complaint (August 2022) said its
   technician understood that I "tacitly agreed to the repair." It does not say a price was quoted, or that
   anyone asked me to approve a repair. The same response says that when I paid, I "stated that it was a
   'partial payment.'"

e) In an August 2022 letter from the creditor to the collection agency, which the agency's attorney sent me from the agency's file: "Finally, sticker shock is not a valid reason
   for not paying a debt owed."

6. THE COUNTERCLAIM
-------------------
Three counts under the FDCPA, all based on the May 2026 lawsuit:
  1. 15 U.S.C. 1692f(1): collecting an amount not expressly authorized by the agreement creating the debt or
     permitted by law.
  2. 15 U.S.C. 1692e(2)(A): false representation of the character, amount, or legal status of the debt.
  3. 15 U.S.C. 1692e(10): false representation or deceptive means to collect a debt.
Relief sought includes statutory damages, costs, and attorney fees.


7. WHERE THE CASE STANDS
------------------------
- Pleadings closed. The agency answered the counterclaim in August 2026. It admits:
    - that it is a "debt collector" under 15 U.S.C. 1692a(6), and that the obligation is a consumer "debt"
      under 1692a(5) (the service was for my personal residence);
    - the assignment, and the invoice's printed policy;
    - that its file has held my 2022 dispute and the creditor's AG response since 2022.
- The agency's pleaded defenses: failure to state a claim; bona fide error (1692k(c)); the debt is valid and
  its statements relied on the creditor's records; good-faith reliance on the creditor; ratification or
  estoppel (that I permitted the work, accepted its benefit, and made a partial payment without objecting
  before the work was done); no actual damages; and the one-year limitations period.
- Discovery. I served two sets of interrogatories, requests for production, and requests for admission. The
  agency served no responses or objections to either set. Beyond the invoice's contents (section 4), the first
  set's requests for admission asked the agency to admit that the only price quoted before the service was the
  diagnostic fee, and that it has no contract, work authorization, or estimate signed by me, and no document
  showing a flat repair fee was presented.
- Motions to compel. Two are pending, set for hearing in early 2027.
- Creditor subpoena. A records subpoena was personally served on the creditor in August 2026. Production was
  incomplete; my motion to compel compliance is set for hearing in early 2027.


8. HOW COUNSEL IS PAID
----------------------
The FDCPA shifts fees to the collector. In "any successful action," the court awards "the costs of the action,
together with a reasonable attorney's fee as determined by the court" (15 U.S.C. 1692k(a)(3)). As I understand
it, courts treat that award as mandatory for a successful consumer. The court sets the amount:
reasonable hours times a reasonable rate, which it may adjust for the results obtained. The fee isn't limited
to the size of the damages; statutory damages are capped at $1,000. If the action succeeds, the agency pays
the fee.
