Case summary

A consumer debt-collection suit with an FDCPA counterclaim, pending in Ada County, Idaho (Fourth Judicial District, Magistrate Division). Bench trial; no trial date set yet. Dollar amounts are rounded for privacy; exact figures are in the full case file.

A note on where I stand: I've filed the pleadings, discovery, and motions to the best of my ability, but I'm not a lawyer, and I know it. I want an attorney to take the lead from here. I'll support the work in any way that helps, and I'll defer to their judgment.

The story

In July 2022 I called an HVAC company to diagnose my air conditioner and agreed to a diagnostic fee of about $100. On that call, after I described the problem, the person I spoke with said it sounded like it could be the thermostat. I said I didn't think so, because the problem had only just started.

The next day, a technician showed up and found the issue was a disconnected thermostat wire, reconnected it, and afterwards came to me and told me he was done. I thanked him, and he said they would bill me.

About two weeks later, I received an invoice for $500. I figured it was a mistake. I had only agreed to a diagnostic, and I assumed reconnecting the thermostat wire was part of it, since it takes about as much time and effort as plugging something into a wall outlet, and I wasn't told about a separate repair or charge before or after the technician did the work.

I called the company to sort out the mix-up. The owner told me, as I wrote at the time, that the reason he charged me $500 was that on that first call I'd told him I didn't think the problem was the thermostat. I described it in writing to both the creditor and the collection agency.

I filed a complaint with the Idaho Attorney General, paid $100 in person, and disputed the rest. The creditor assigned the roughly $400 balance to a collection agency. On authorization, its written response to the Attorney General offered one explanation: that its technician understood I had "tacitly agreed to the repair." The Attorney General's office then closed the complaint, noting that the business was unwilling to resolve it.

I sent the agency a detailed written dispute in August 2022, pointing to that response. Its answer to my counterclaim admits forwarding that dispute to the creditor. In 2026 the agency and its attorney sent new demands; I disputed again in writing. The attorney replied with a copy of the invoice and sued nearly four years later, in May 2026.

The creditor's own words

The creditor's invoice describes its own process: a technician diagnoses the problem, then presents the problem and a flat fee to repair it, and no diagnostic fee is charged if the customer approves the repair at the time of the diagnostic.

No flat fee was presented to me, and I was not asked to approve a repair. When I complained to the Idaho Attorney General in July 2022, the creditor's written response offered one explanation on authorization: that its technician understood that I "tacitly agreed to the repair." It does not say a price was quoted, or that anyone asked me to approve a repair.

In an August 2022 letter from the creditor to the collection agency, which the agency's attorney sent me from the agency's file: "Finally, sticker shock is not a valid reason for not paying a debt owed."

The updated invoice the creditor emailed me in July 2022 lists the diagnostic at $0.00, although its policy waives that fee only when a customer approves the repair. It also lists finding and correcting the wiring problem as one of the diagnostic tasks, and then separately charges about $500 on a wiring-repair line item.

The counterclaim

Three counts under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, all based on the May 2026 lawsuit:

  1. § 1692f(1): collecting an amount not expressly authorized by the agreement creating the debt or permitted by law.
  2. § 1692e(2)(A): false representation of the character, amount, or legal status of the debt.
  3. § 1692e(10): false representation or deceptive means to collect a debt.

Relief sought includes statutory damages, costs, and attorney fees.

Where the case stands

It admits it is a debt collector under § 1692a(6), and admits the assignment, the invoice's policy, and that its file has held my 2022 dispute and the creditor's AG response since 2022.   • Creditor subpoena. A records subpoena was personally served on the creditor in August 2026. Production was incomplete; my motion to compel compliance is set for hearing in early 2027.

How counsel is paid

The FDCPA shifts fees to the collector. In "any successful action," the court awards "the costs of the action, together with a reasonable attorney's fee as determined by the court" (15 U.S.C. § 1692k(a)(3)). As I understand it, courts treat that award as mandatory for a successful consumer. The court sets the amount: reasonable hours times a reasonable rate, which it may adjust for the results obtained. The fee isn't limited to the size of the damages; statutory damages are capped at $1,000. If the action succeeds, the agency pays the fee.

Questions you'll have

Isn't this just a billing dispute?

The creditor's written explanation to the Attorney General in 2022 is that its technician understood I "tacitly agreed." I was quoted no price and signed nothing. The agency has held that statement, and my written dispute, since 2022.

Is there a signed work authorization?

No. I signed nothing, approved no repair, and was never quoted a repair price.

Can an out-of-state attorney take this?

An Idaho-licensed attorney anywhere can appear directly. An out-of-state attorney can appear pro hac vice with Idaho local counsel (I.B.C.R. 227); the court may excuse local counsel from attending hearings.
